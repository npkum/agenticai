Introduction

While much of the conversation around Agentic AI focuses on its capabilities and potential benefits, it is equally important to understand where its use may be unsuitable. Deploying LLM-driven AI agents in the wrong context—such as areas requiring immediate real-time control, high-stakes human judgment, or guaranteed transparency—can introduce operational, compliance, or reputational risks that outweigh the benefits. By identifying these “do-not-use” scenarios in advance, organizations can make informed, risk-aware decisions and ensure that AI adoption aligns with both business objectives and governance standards.

Key Factors in the Agentic AI Suitability Framework

When deciding to use Agentic AI for decision-making (e.g., using LLMs to make autonomous choices), these factors help to identify scenarios with high risk or low business value:

  • Operational Costs
  • Human Impact
  • Real-time Requirements
  • Transparency
  • Integration Complexity
  • Data Sensitivity
  • Governance Requirements
  • Security Implications

Why These Factors Matter for Deciding Agentic AI

1. Operational Costs

AI agents, especially LLM-powered ones, can incur high operational expenses from compute, API calls, retraining, and integration. In high-frequency or always-on decision-making, these costs can quickly exceed the business value delivered.

2. Human Impact

Some interactions demand emotional intelligence, empathy, and trust-building—qualities AI still cannot truly replicate. Removing humans from these touchpoints can erode customer relationships and harm brand reputation.

3. Real-time Requirements

LLM-based agents are not optimized for ultra-low latency, high-throughput environments. In scenarios where milliseconds matter (e.g., manufacturing controls, high-frequency trading), delayed or inconsistent responses can cause safety hazards or losses.

4. Transparency

Agentic AI may make decisions without clear, traceable reasoning. This is a serious concern in regulated industries or customer-facing decisions, where explanations are essential for compliance and trust.

5. Integration Complexity

Many enterprises operate on a patchwork of legacy and modern systems. Complex integrations can cause delays, higher costs, and unstable performance, especially when AI needs to operate across multiple platforms.

6. Data Sensitivity

AI agents are only as good as the data they rely on. Incomplete, outdated, or biased data leads to flawed decisions, wasted resources, and possible compliance violations.

7. Governance Requirements

Without clear policies, monitoring, and escalation paths, AI agents can take actions that conflict with company rules, legal requirements, or ethical standards—sometimes without anyone noticing until it’s too late.

8. Security Implications

Autonomous decision-making opens new attack surfaces. If an AI agent has access to sensitive systems, attackers may trick it into unsafe actions or extract confidential data.

Critical Factors for Assessing Agentic AI Fit

1. Costs

Scenario: A mid-sized retail chain uses an LLM-powered AI agent to autonomously negotiate supplier contracts. The agent incurs high API usage costs from constant model queries, plus large expenses for integration and retraining.

Risk: Agentic AI can create runaway operational costs if decision-making is continuous, high-frequency, or data-intensive.

Alternative: Use a rules-based automation system for routine contract renewals and only involve a human in complex negotiations.

Mitigation: Implement cost-governance controls — limit API calls, use budget caps, and introduce checkpoints where a human must approve further actions.

2. Human Impact

Scenario:A hospital replaces its patient liaison role with an AI agent that autonomously handles complaint resolution and emotional support for families.

Risk: AI can’t truly empathize, read subtle emotional cues, or build trust in sensitive situations — harming relationships.

Alternative: Use AI only as a support tool — suggest responses, provide quick facts, or prepare case summaries — while a human leads the conversation.

Mitigation: Set boundaries where AI escalates to a human for emotionally charged or sensitive topics, ensuring human oversight in final communications.

3. Real-time Requirements

Scenario: An AI agent is used to control a manufacturing robot on a high-speed assembly line, making split-second adjustments based on sensor data.

Risk:LLM-based decision-making has latency and may not process millisecond-level events reliably, risking safety and production quality.

Alternative:Use real-time control systems (e.g., PLCs (Programmable Logic Controllers) or edge AI models) that are optimized for low latency and deterministic performance.

Mitigation:Limit its role to offline optimization — analysing production logs to suggest efficiency improvements — rather than controlling real-time actions.

4. Transparency

Scenario:A financial services firm uses an AI agent to autonomously approve or reject small business loan applications.

Risk: LLM-based decisions can be hard to explain, making it impossible to justify rejections to regulators or customers.

Alternative: Use structured scoring systems with transparent criteria.

Mitigation: Add decision logging and explanation layers — store reasoning steps, generate human-readable justifications, and enable audit trails.

5. Integration Complexity

Scenario: A logistics company tries to integrate an AI agent into 15 different legacy systems for shipment tracking, customs processing, and invoicing.

Risk: Integration complexity causes delays, data mismatches, and extra maintenance costs, outweighing the benefits.

Alternative: Start with point-specific automation — e.g., AI-assisted invoice processing — before attempting multi-system autonomy.

Mitigation: Adopt modular integration — connect the agent through APIs or middleware that can be swapped without touching all legacy systems at once.

6. Data Sensitivity

Scenario:A marketing firm’s AI agent uses incomplete customer profiles and outdated sales data to generate automated ad campaigns.

Risk: Poor data quality leads to wrong targeting, wasted spend, and reputational damage.

Alternative:Use data cleansing and validation pipelines before AI involvement; consider simpler analytics for low-confidence datasets.

Mitigation: Implement data quality gates — the agent should verify freshness, completeness, and accuracy before acting on data.

7. Governance Requirements

Scenario: A startup launches a sales AI agent that autonomously emails prospects, without rules on tone, frequency, or audience selection. It accidentally spams VIP clients with irrelevant offers.

Risk: Without governance, AI agents can violate policies, laws, or ethics, damaging brand and legal standing.

Alternative: Create human-led approval workflows for outbound actions until governance frameworks are in place.

Mitigation: Implement agent policy enforcement — rules, monitoring dashboards, and human-in-the-loop checkpoints for high-impact actions.

8. Security Implications

Scenario: A customer support AI agent autonomously resets passwords for user accounts after minimal verification, leaving the system open to social engineering attacks.

Risk:Autonomous agents can be exploited if attackers trick them into unsafe actions.

Alternative: Keep critical security processes manual or semi-automated with multi-factor human verification.

Mitigation: Enforce strict role-based access control, sandbox the agent, and add anomaly detection for suspicious requests.

Guidance

The decision to use Agentic AI should be guided entirely by your specific use case, risk tolerance, and operational context.

The scenarios provided here are not blanket prohibitions—they illustrate environments where the risks can outweigh the benefits.

In some cases, non-agentic approaches (rules-based automation, traditional AI models, robotic process automation (RPA)) may deliver safer, more cost-effective results. In others, Agentic AI can still be valuable if combined with strong governance, clear escalation rules, and robust security measures.

Agentic AI can deliver significant value, but it must be deployed with appropriate safeguards, governance, and alignment to the intended use case.

Framework

Purpose

To guide informed decision-making by identifying conditions where Agentic AI may pose excessive risk, cost, or operational challenges—ensuring adoption is intentional, governed, and aligned with business priorities.

Step 1 – Define the Use Case

  • Describe the objective—What decision or task will the AI agent perform?
  • Identify criticality—Is it mission-critical, customer-facing, or regulatory-sensitive?
  • Determine autonomy level—Will AI act independently or with human oversight?

Step 2 – Assess Against Key Risk Factors

Factor Key Assessment Questions Considerations When Agentic AI May Not Be the Optimal Approach
Costs What is the projected API, compute, and integration cost vs ROI? High operational costs outweigh expected benefits
Human Impact Does the task require empathy, trust-building, or complex social judgment? Removal of human involvement would degrade quality of interaction
Real-time Requirements Does it require millisecond-level decision-making or low-latency control? AI cannot meet performance demands consistently
Lack of Transparency Is explainability required for compliance or customer trust? AI decisions are not easily interpretable
Integration Complexity Does it require connecting to multiple legacy or siloed systems? Integration costs and risks are excessive
Data Sensitivity Is the data complete, accurate, current, and unbiased? Data quality is insufficient or uncertain
Governance Requirements Are there clear policies, monitoring, and escalation processes? No governance framework in place
Security Implications Could the AI’s access or actions be exploited by attackers? Security risks are unacceptably high

Step 3 – Decide the Path Forward

  • If one or more risk factors are “High” and unavoidable: Consider non-agentic alternatives (rules-based automation, traditional AI, human-led workflows).
  • If risks are “Moderate” and manageable: Proceed with safeguards—apply governance, human-in-loop controls, data validation, and security measures.
  • If risks are “Low”: Agentic AI can be deployed with standard operational controls.

Step 4 – Governance and Monitoring

  • Establish continuous oversight—monitor AI behavior, costs, and performance.
  • Define escalation protocols for unexpected or high-impact AI actions.
  • Conduct periodic risk reviews—re-evaluate suitability as technology and business context evolve.

Takeaway

Understanding where not to use Agentic AI is as important as knowing where it can succeed. This framework ensures adoption decisions are based on a balanced evaluation of capability vs. risk, avoiding costly missteps and aligning AI use with organizational strategy.
